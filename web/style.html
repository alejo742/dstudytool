The desk's real question

Every question on these tests is a thin disguise for one question: can this person make a good decision with incomplete information, quickly, and without embarrassing themselves on arithmetic? A market maker quotes hundreds of prices a day. Each quote is a tiny bet whose edge is a fraction of a cent, so what pays is being right slightly more often than wrong, fast, and never catastrophically wrong.

That is why the tests look the way they do. Mental arithmetic checks that the machinery of numbers is automatic. Probability and expected value check that you reason in bets rather than in stories. Brainteasers and pattern questions check that you can find structure you were not shown. Coding questions check that you can turn an idea into something that runs correctly under a clock. Everything else is a variation on these four.

Why speed is a filter, not a talent

On a desk you never get a calculator moment. You quote, you hedge, you decide, and the numbers have to already be in your head. Under a clock, working memory is the bottleneck: if you spend it on 47 × 23, you have nothing left for the actual problem. The goal of this module is to turn arithmetic into pattern recognition so that your attention stays free.

Two things make it trainable. First, a small set of identities covers most of what appears. Second, speed comes from repetition on fresh problems, which is why the drill below generates endless ones. Ten focused minutes a day beats a two-hour weekend session.

Benchmark. On the standard 120-second online arithmetic game (addition and subtraction up to 100, multiplication of 2–12 by up to 100, matching division), rough community numbers are: 20 to 30 when you start, 40 to 50 when comfortable, 60 or more is strong, 80 or more is elite. Treat these as signposts, not promises about any particular test.

The anchor method for numbers in the teens and twenties

Pick an anchor A near both numbers (15, 20 or 25) and write each number as the anchor plus or minus a small offset: a = A + x and b = A + y. Then

(A + x)(A + y) = A × (A + x + y) + xy

In words: add the offsets to the anchor, multiply by the anchor, then add the product of the offsets (two negative offsets multiply to a positive number). It is the near-base trick from earlier with a base of 20 instead of 100.

Why consecutive squares differ by an odd number
Each new square wraps an L-shaped border around the last one, and the L has 2n + 1 cells. So from 25² = 625 you climb by 51, 53, 55, 57, 59 to reach 30² = 900.
  • The ladder. (n + 1)² = n² + 2n + 1. From 25² = 625: 26² = 625 + 51 = 676, 27² = 676 + 53 = 729, 28² = 729 + 55 = 784, 29² = 784 + 57 = 841, 30² = 841 + 59 = 900.

  • Recognising perfect squares. A square ends in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6 or 9 and never in 2, 3, 7 or 8. Its digital root is 1, 4, 7 or 9. Those two checks reject most non-squares at a glance.
  • Cubes to 12 (1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216, 343, 512, 729, 1000, 1331, 1728) and the powers of 2 up to 216 come up often enough to be worth owning.

Percentages, division shortcuts and percent changes

Division by 5, 25 and 125 is multiplication by 2, 4 and 8 followed by a shift of the decimal point. 480 ÷ 12.5 = 480 × 8 ÷ 100 = 38.4. Fractions you should recognise on sight as percentages: 1/8 = 12.5%, 3/8 = 37.5%, 5/8 = 62.5%, 7/8 = 87.5%, and the sixths (16.67%, 33.3%, 66.7%, 83.3%).

  • Swap the percentage: x% of y equals y% of x. 8% of 25 is 25% of 8, which is 2.
  • Build from 10%: 15% = 10% + half of that. 2.5% = a quarter of 10%. So 2.5% of 1840 is 184 ÷ 4 = 46.
  • Compare fractions by cross-multiplying: a/b versus c/d is ad versus bc. If they are close, that product is your only reliable tool.

Percent changes compose by multiplication

A gain of a followed by a gain of b is (1 + a)(1 + b) − 1 = a + b + ab. Up 10% then down 10% is 1.1 × 0.9 − 1 = −1%. Up x% then down x% always loses about x²/100 percentage points. A 50% loss needs a 100% gain to recover.

Trap. "Percent" and "percentage points" are different. A rate moving from 4% to 5% is up 1 percentage point and up 25 percent. And to undo a 20% increase you divide by 1.2, you do not subtract 20%.

Approximation: the two-significant-figure habit

Exact arithmetic is slower than estimation, and most tests only need the estimate. On a multiple-choice test, answers usually differ by more than 5%; in an interview, a number within 1% to 3% with an honest error band is better than an exact number after a long silence. The habit has three moves:

  1. Round to two significant figures (or to a friendly number such as 50, 25 or 7/40) and compute with the rounded numbers.
  2. Track the direction of each rounding. If you rounded a factor up, your product is high; if you rounded the denominator up, your quotient is low.
  3. Correct if the answer is close to a boundary between options, using the size of the roundings.

How errors combine

Suppose each input is off by a small relative error ε. Then relative errors add in a product, subtract in a quotient (the denominator's error enters with the opposite sign), halve under a square root, and multiply by the exponent in a power. If A is rounded up 3%, B down 2% and C up 4%, then A × B ÷ C is off by about +3 − 2 − 4 = −3% (exactly −2.9%). This also tells you how to round: round the numerator and the denominator in the same direction, and the factors of a product in opposite directions, so the errors cancel.

Estimation: roots, logs and compounding

Roots by one Newton step

To get √N, guess g, then average g with N/g. For √2000: guess 45, 2000/45 ≈ 44.44, average 44.72. One step roughly doubles your correct digits. For numbers just off a square, √(a² + r) ≈ a + r/(2a).

Logs and powers of ten

Keep log₁₀2 = 0.301, log₁₀3 = 0.477, log₁₀7 = 0.845, and ln 2 = 0.693, ln 10 = 2.303. Then 210 = 1024 ≈ 103 gives 2100 ≈ 1030, and the number of digits of 2n is ⌊0.301n⌋ + 1. To compare two huge powers, compare their logs.

Compounding and the rule of 72

For small x, (1 + x)n ≈ 1 + nx + n(n − 1)x²/2. For longer horizons use en ln(1 + x) with ln(1 + x) ≈ x − x²/2. Doubling time at r% a year is about 72/r years: 8% doubles in 9 years (the exact answer is 9.006).

Estimating is a skill with a feedback loop: always say your answer, then say how wrong it could be. "About 45, give or take one" is a real answer.

Tactics for numeric reasoning tests

  • Bound before you compute. If the options are 1,210, 1,320, 1,540 and 2,000, a rough estimate decides it.
  • Use the last digit. The units digit of a product depends only on the units digits of the factors. Four options with four different last digits are free points.
  • Keep a rhythm. If a question stalls past twice your average, skip it. One lost question costs less than three you never reach.
  • Read the units and the question. Per cent versus per mille, thousands versus millions, "which is smaller" versus "which is larger".

Count arrangements, selections and paths fluently, recognise the standard models (stars and bars, inclusion–exclusion, derangements, Catalan), and turn counts into probabilities.

Two rules and one habit

The product rule: if a task is done in stages and stage i can be done in ni ways regardless of earlier choices, there are n1n2··· ways in total. The sum rule: if a task falls into disjoint cases, add the cases. Everything else is bookkeeping.

The habit that separates fast counters from slow ones is choosing the order of decisions so each stage's count does not depend on earlier choices. How many 3-digit numbers have all digits distinct? Choose the hardest-constrained digit first: the hundreds digit has 9 options (not 0), the tens digit has 9 (anything but the hundreds digit, now 0 is allowed), the units digit has 8. That gives 9 × 9 × 8 = 648. Choose the units digit first and the count of the hundreds digit depends on whether you used 0, and the arithmetic gets ugly.

Permutations and combinations. Ordered selections of k from n: n!/(n−k)!. Unordered selections: nk = n!/(k!(n−k)!). If you can relabel the objects and the outcome is unchanged, divide by the symmetry. That one sentence derives every formula in this module.

Every entry is the sum of the two above it. In words: to choose k items from n, either the last item is in (choose k−1 from the rest) or it is out (choose k). Click around and watch it happen.

Stars and bars

How many ways can n identical objects go into k distinct boxes? Line up n stars and insert k−1 bars to separate the boxes: a string of n + k − 1 symbols where you choose which are bars.

The encoding
Counts. Non-negative solutions to x1 + ··· + xk = n: n+k−1k−1. Positive solutions: n−1k−1 (give each box one object first). Upper bounds are handled by inclusion–exclusion: count all, subtract the ones that violate a bound, add back overlaps.

Inclusion–exclusion and derangements

Three sets, seven regions
Each region is counted once after the alternating corrections. In general: add singles, subtract pairs, add triples, and so on.

Example: how many integers from 1 to 100 are divisible by none of 2, 3, 5? Singles 50 + 33 + 20 = 103, pairs 16 + 10 + 6 = 32, triple 3. So 100 − 103 + 32 − 3 = 26.

Derangements

A derangement is a permutation with no fixed point (nobody gets their own hat). Inclusion–exclusion over "person i gets their own hat" gives Dn = n! ∑k=0n (−1)k/k!, which is the nearest integer to n!/e. So the probability that nobody gets their own hat tends to 1/e ≈ 0.368, and it is already 0.3667 at n = 5. Memorise the sequence 1, 0, 1, 2, 9, 44, 265, 1854, 14833, 133496 for n = 0 to 9, and the recurrence Dn = (n−1)(Dn−1 + Dn−2).

Lattice paths, reflection and Catalan numbers

Paths from (0,0) to (a, b) using unit steps right and up number a+ba, since you choose which of the steps go right. Now forbid crossing a boundary. The reflection principle counts the bad paths: reflect the part of a bad path before its first touch of the forbidden line, and you get a bijection with all paths to a reflected endpoint. For paths from (0,0) to (n,n) that never go above the diagonal, the count is 2nn − 2nn+1 = 2nn/(n+1), the Catalan number Cn.

Count paths to every node
Ballot theorem. In an election where A gets a votes and B gets b < a, the probability that A is strictly ahead at every point of the count is (a−b)/(a+b). The Catalan numbers 1, 1, 2, 5, 14, 42, 132, 429, 1430 count balanced parenthesisations, binary trees with n nodes and triangulations of a convex (n+2)-gon.

From counting to probability: the poker table

With equally likely outcomes, probability is a ratio of counts. Poker is the training ground: count hands by choosing the ranks, then the suits, then the kickers. The total is 525 = 2,598,960. The recipe for a full house: choose the rank of the three (13), choose which three suits (43 = 4), choose the rank of the pair (12), choose its suits (42 = 6): 13 × 4 × 12 × 6 = 3,744.

3Probability foundations

Set up a problem cleanly, condition correctly, apply Bayes without panic, and recognise the famous traps before they get you.

Time 6 hoursPrerequisites Module 2Feeds Modules 4 to 9

Three questions before any calculation

  1. What is the sample space, and are its points equally likely? Most wrong answers come from treating unequal outcomes as equal ("either I win or I lose, so 50%").
  2. What exactly am I conditioning on? Write the condition down in symbols. "At least one is a boy" and "the older one is a boy" are different events.
  3. Is there a symmetry or linearity shortcut? If a short argument gives the answer, a long computation is just a chance to make an arithmetic mistake.

The axioms need only two working consequences: P(not A) = 1 − P(A), and P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). The complement is your best friend whenever the phrase "at least one" appears.

Conditioning, independence and total probability

Definitions. P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B). Events are independent if P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B). Law of total probability: if B1, ..., Bk partition the space, then P(A) = ∑ P(A | Bi)P(Bi).
Trap. Independent and mutually exclusive are almost opposites. If A and B cannot both happen and both have positive probability, knowing A happened tells you B did not, so they are very dependent.

Bayes' rule, desk version

Textbooks write P(H | E) = P(E | H)P(H)/P(E). On the desk we use the odds form, because it turns updating into multiplication:

Posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio. Prior odds of H versus not-H are P(H)/P(not H). The likelihood ratio is P(E | H)/P(E | not H), how much more likely the evidence is if H is true.

A 99%-sensitive, 99%-specific test for a condition with 1% prevalence: prior odds 1 : 99, likelihood ratio 0.99/0.01 = 99, posterior odds 99 : 99 = 1 : 1, so P(sick | positive) = 50%. A great test, a rare condition, a coin flip of an answer. Play with it:

Base rates in 2,000 people
Trap: the prosecutor's fallacy. "The chance of this DNA match if he is innocent is one in a million" is P(evidence | innocent). The jury needs P(innocent | evidence). In a city of ten million, about ten innocent people also match, so without other evidence the posterior is roughly one in ten, not one in a million. The same mistake appears as "this signal had a 1% chance of appearing by luck, so it is 99% real". Always ask for the base rate.

Monty Hall

Three doors, one prize. You pick one; the host, who knows where the prize is, opens another door showing a goat and offers a switch. Your first pick was right 1/3 of the time and the host's action does not change that. So the remaining unopened door holds the prize 2/3 of the time. The cleanest way to believe it: with 100 doors, you pick one, and the host opens 98 goats. Would you really stay with your 1% pick? Try the game, then change the rules.

Monty Hall lab

Notice what the "host opens just one door" variant does with many doors: the benefit of switching shrinks, because you must now choose among several unopened doors. The knowledge of the host is the entire game.

Symmetry beats computation

Shuffle a deck and look at the 37th card. What is the probability it is an ace? Exactly 4/52 = 1/13. Every position is equally likely to hold any given card, so you need not compute anything about the first 36. The same argument shows that the first card and the last card are equally likely to be the ace of spades, and that two specific positions are equally likely to hold any ordered pair of distinct cards. This is exchangeability, and it is the source of the fastest solutions on any probability test.

A related idea: for any two independent identically distributed continuous random variables, P(X < Y) = 1/2 by symmetry, and P(X1 is the largest of n) = 1/n.

The birthday paradox

With n people and D equally likely birthdays, the probability that everybody is different is ∏i=0n−1(1 − i/D). Taking logs, ln(1 − i/D) ≈ −i/D, and summing gives the key approximation:

P(some pair shares a birthday) ≈ 1 − e−n(n−1)/(2D), so 50% arrives near n ≈ 1.18√D.

The reason it feels paradoxical is that there are n2 pairs, and pairs grow quadratically. With 23 people there are 253 pairs, each with a 1/365 chance. For D = 365 the exact numbers are 50.7% at 23 people, 70.6% at 30, 89.1% at 40, and 97.0% at 50. A three-way match needs about 88 people for even odds.

Birthday collisions
Whenever a problem asks "how many items until a repeat", think square root of the number of possibilities. It is the hash-collision rule, the reason 64-bit IDs are not unique forever, and a favourite interview opener.

Geometric probability

When outcomes are continuous and uniform, probability is area (or length, or volume). The recipe: choose coordinates, write the event as inequalities, draw the region, compute its area.

Break a stick at two random points. What is the probability the three pieces form a triangle? Let the cut points be x < y in [0,1] (the other ordering is symmetric). The pieces are x, y−x, 1−y. A triangle exists exactly when each piece is shorter than 1/2: x < 1/2, y > 1/2, y−x < 1/2.

The favourable region

The answer is 1/4. Notice how the uniform measure turned a probability into a drawing. The meeting problem is the same idea: two people arrive uniformly in an hour and each waits 15 minutes; they meet iff |x − y| ≤ 1/4, and the square minus two corner triangles of side 3/4 has area 1 − (3/4)2 = 7/16.

4Expectation, variance and linearity

Turn counting-heavy problems into one-line sums with indicators, compute variances without fuss, and price waiting-time and stopping games.

The two formulas you will use for years

Expectation. E[X] = ∑ x P(X = x). For any function, E[g(X)] = ∑ g(x) P(X = x) (you do not need the distribution of g(X)). Linearity: E[aX + bY] = aE[X] + bE[Y] always, whether or not X and Y are independent.

Linearity is the single most useful fact in this guide. The trick that unlocks it is the indicator: let Ij = 1 if event j happens and 0 otherwise. Then E[Ij] = P(event j), and any count is a sum of indicators, so its expectation is a sum of probabilities. No distribution of the count is ever needed.

Tail-sum formula. For a non-negative integer variable, E[X] = ∑k≥1 P(X ≥ k). For continuous non-negative X, E[X] = ∫0∞ P(X > t) dt. Use it when P(X ≥ k) is easy: for the maximum of two dice, P(max ≥ k) = 1 − ((k−1)/6)2, so E[max] = 6 − (0 + 1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25)/36 = 161/36 ≈ 4.47.

Variance in one page

Variance. Var(X) = E[X2] − E[X]2 = E[(X − μ)2]. Var(aX + b) = a2Var(X). Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2Cov(X, Y), and Cov = 0 when independent. Standard deviation is the square root.
  • An indicator with probability p has variance p(1 − p). A binomial is a sum of independent indicators, so Var = np(1 − p).
  • One fair die: E = 7/2, E[X2] = 91/6, so Var = 91/6 − 49/4 = 35/12 ≈ 2.92. A sum of n dice has variance 35n/12; for 10 dice, sd ≈ 5.40.
  • Fixed points of a random permutation have mean and variance 1 (for n ≥ 2). They behave like a Poisson(1).
  • Uncorrelated does not mean independent: for X symmetric about 0, Cov(X, X2) = E[X3] − E[X]E[X2] = 0, yet X2 is a function of X.

Waiting times and patterns

A fair coin is flipped until the pattern HH appears. The expected number of flips is 6. For HT it is 4. The same coin, the same frequency of both patterns (each appears in a quarter of all positions), yet different waits: HH can overlap itself, so after a near miss you restart from scratch, while HT cannot.

The overlap rule. For a pattern of length L, E[wait] = ∑ 2k over every k from 1 to L for which the first k symbols equal the last k symbols. HH: 2 + 4 = 6. HT: only the whole pattern, 4. HTH: 2 + 8 = 10. HHH: 2 + 4 + 8 = 14. For n heads in a row: 2n+1 − 2. (Module 5 explains why it works.)

Order statistics and the uniform shortcut

Drop n independent uniform points on [0, 1]. They cut the interval into n + 1 gaps that are symmetric (exchangeable), so each has expected length 1/(n + 1). Gambler's ruin

You hold i dollars and bet one dollar at a time, winning each bet with probability p, until you hit N or go broke. Let q = 1 − p and r = q/p.

Fair bets (p = 1/2): P(reach N) = i/N, and the expected number of bets is i(N − i). Unfair bets: P(reach N) = (1 − ri)/(1 − rN), and the expected duration is i/(q − p) − (N/(q − p))·P(reach N).

The derivation is first-step analysis: f(i) = p f(i+1) + q f(i−1) with f(0) = 0 and f(N) = 1. Fair bets make f linear. Try the widget with p = 0.47: a tiny edge against you is brutal when the target is far.

Martingales: the shortcut

A martingale is a process whose expected next value, given everything so far, equals its current value: a fair game. The optional stopping theorem says that for a martingale and a reasonable stopping time T, E[MT] = M0. (Reasonable: T has finite expectation and the process is bounded, or similar.) Three uses:

  1. Ruin probability for fair bets. Wealth Sn is a martingale. At stopping, ST is 0 or N: i = E[ST] = N·P(reach N). So P = i/N with no recursion.
  2. Expected duration. Sn2 − n is a martingale for a fair ±1 walk. Optional stopping: i2 = E[ST2] − E[T] = N2·(i/N) − E[T], so E[T] = i(N − i).
  3. Patterns (the casino argument). Before each flip a new gambler arrives and bets $1 that this flip starts the pattern, reinvesting each win on the next letter at fair odds. Every bet is fair, so the casino's expected profit is zero: expected money collected, E[T] dollars (one per arrival), equals expected money paid out at time T. The only gamblers still holding money at T are those whose partial pattern lines up with the end, which is exactly the prefix = suffix overlap list. For HH: gamblers hold 22 + 21 = 6. For ABRACADABRA with a 26-letter alphabet: 2611 + 264 + 26 = 3,670,344,487,444,778 expected letters.

Penney's game: non-transitive coins

Surprisingly, whatever you choose, I can pick a pattern that wins more than half the time. The recipe: if you pick x y z, I pick (not y) x y. Against HHH I pick THH and win 7 times out of 8. Markov chains in a nutshell

A Markov chain has a state and a transition matrix P with Pij = P(next = j | now = i). The distribution after n steps is the initial row vector times Pn. Exponential and Poisson: the memoryless pair

Events arrive at rate λ per unit time, independently. Then the count in a window of length t is Poisson(λt) and the waiting time to the next event is Exponential(λ). Four facts do most of the work:

  1. Memorylessness. P(X > s + t | X > s) = P(X > t) = e−λt. If you have already waited an hour for the bus, the expected remaining wait is unchanged.
  2. Competing clocks. For independent Exponential(λ1) and Exponential(λ2): the minimum is Exponential(λ1 + λ2), and P(X1 < X2) = λ1/(λ1 + λ2).
  3. Thinning and merging. Keep each event independently with probability p: the kept events form a Poisson process of rate λp (and the discarded ones an independent process of rate λ(1 − p)). Two independent processes merge into one with rate λ1 + λ2.
  4. Given the count, the times are uniform. If exactly n events happened in [0, T], their times are n iid uniforms on [0, T].
The waiting-time paradox. Buses arrive as a Poisson process, on average every 10 minutes. You arrive at a random time. Your expected wait is 10 minutes (memorylessness), and the expected time since the last bus is also 10 minutes, so the gap you land in averages 20 minutes, twice the average gap. Long gaps are over-represented in whatever you randomly sample (size-biased sampling). The same effect makes your friends have more friends than you, and your trades be on the wrong side of long moves.

Transformations, joints, and tools

  • CDF method. For a monotone transformation, find P(Y ≤ y) in terms of the CDF of X, then differentiate. If U ~ U(0,1), then −ln U ~ Exp(1); and for any continuous CDF F, F−1(U) has CDF F (inverse transform sampling, the basis of every Monte Carlo generator).
  • Max and min. For iid with CDF F: P(max ≤ x) = F(x)n and P(min > x) = (1 − F(x))n.
  • Moment generating functions. M(t) = E[etX]. Moments are derivatives at 0, MGFs of independent sums multiply, and so a sum of independent Poissons is Poisson, and a sum of independent normals is normal. The CLT proof is an MGF limit.
  • Inequalities. Markov: P(X ≥ a) ≤ E[X]/a for X ≥ 0. Chebyshev: P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k2. Jensen: E[f(X)] ≥ f(E[X]) for convex f (so E[X2] ≥ E[X]2, and the average of exp is above exp of the average). Cauchy–Schwarz: |Cov(X, Y)| ≤ σXσY, so correlation lies in [−1, 1].

EV is the compass, bankroll is the road

If a bet has positive expected value and the stake is small relative to your bankroll, take it. That one rule decides most interview questions. The subtlety enters when you repeat bets and ruin is possible: maximising expected wealth would tell you to bet everything on every positive-edge bet, which goes broke with certainty in the long run. The resolution is to maximise the growth rate of wealth, which is the expected log of the growth factor per bet.

The Kelly criterion

You can bet a fraction f of your bankroll on an event that wins with probability p and pays b to 1 (and loses the stake otherwise). After a win your wealth is multiplied by 1 + bf, after a loss by 1 − f. The long-run growth rate is

g(f) = p ln(1 + bf) + (1 − p) ln(1 − f)

Setting the derivative to zero gives the Kelly fraction f* = (bp − q)/b = p − q/b with q = 1 − p. For an even-money bet this is 2p − 1: edge divided by odds. Explore the curve and watch what over-betting does.

Kelly growth rate and 20 simulated bettors
  • The curve is flat near f* and falls off a cliff to the right. Betting double Kelly gives roughly zero growth, and anything more is a guaranteed long-run loss even with a positive edge.
  • Half Kelly gets about 75% of the optimal growth with a much smoother ride. With real-world uncertainty about your edge, fractional Kelly is what practitioners actually use.
  • Kelly is the same idea as a trader's risk limit: size so that a bad run cannot end you.

Stopping problems and the martingale trap

Backward induction (Module 4's dice recursion) solves most stopping problems. The classic is the secretary problem: n candidates arrive in random order, you see each once, you must accept or reject immediately, and you want the best. The optimal rule: reject the first r ≈ n/e candidates outright, then take the first one better than all so far. The success probability tends to 1/e ≈ 37%.

Secretary problem

A reverse lesson comes from a game where the intuition says you can win: 26 red and 26 black cards are shuffled and turned over one at a time. At any moment you may stop and bet $1 that the next card is red. Can you beat the deck by choosing when? No. The fraction of red cards remaining is a martingale, so at any stopping rule the probability the next card is red equals 1/2 on average. You cannot time a fair game, which is the same statement as "you cannot time a fairly priced market with no information".

Beauty contest. Everyone picks a number from 0 to 100, and the winner is the player closest to two-thirds of the average. If everyone picks randomly, the average is 50, so you pick 33. But if everyone reasons that way, the average is 33, so you pick 22; then 15, 10, 7, ... and the only stable answer (the Nash equilibrium) is 0. In practice people stop after a step or two. The lesson for a market maker: the right answer depends on how deeply the others think, and you earn money by being one level ahead rather than infinitely deep.

The winner's curse

You are bidding for a company. Its value to the current owner is V, uniform on [0, 100], and unknown to you. Under your management it would be worth 1.5V. The owner sells if your bid is at least V. What should you bid? The seductive argument ("on average it is worth 50 to them and 75 to me, so bid 60") is wrong, because they only accept when you are overpaying. If you bid b, you win when V ≤ b, and then the average value is b/2, worth 0.75b to you. Expected profit is (b/100)(0.75b − b) = −b2/400, negative for every positive bid. The best bid is zero. Conditioning on the event "my bid is accepted" is the heart of adverse selection, and Module 8 will make you pay for forgetting it.

Poker maths for traders

Poker problems are expected-value problems with incomplete information, which is why trading firms use them. Four formulas cover almost everything:

Pot odds

The pot holds P and your opponent bets B. Calling costs B to win P + B. Break-even equity: B/(P + 2B). Pot 100, bet 50: you need 50/200 = 25%.

Outs and equity

With k outs and two cards to come the chance of hitting by the river is 1 − ((47−k)/47)((46−k)/46); rule of thumb 4% per out. With one card to come: k/46, rule of thumb 2% per out.

Bluff frequency

If you bet B into P, the opponent is indifferent about calling when your bluffs make up B/(P + 2B) of your betting range. A pot-sized bet gives 1/3 bluffs, 2/3 value.

Minimum defence

To make your opponent's bluffs break even you must call with probability P/(P + B). Against a pot-sized bet you call half the time.

An auction in one calculation

Two bidders have private values iid U(0, 1). In a second-price auction, bid your value (it is a dominant strategy). In a first-price auction, bidding your value earns zero profit, so you shade. If the opponent bids v′/2, you bid b to win when v′ < 2b, with probability 2b, earning (v − b)(2b), which is maximised at b = v/2. So both bid half their value. Expected revenue is E[max(v1, v2)/2] = 1/3 in the first-price auction and E[min(v1, v2)] = 1/3 in the second-price auction. Same revenue: the revenue equivalence theorem.

A shuffled deck has 26 red and 26 black cards, turned face up one at a time. At any moment you may stop and guess that the next card is red; you win $1 if right. What is the probability of winning under the best strategy? What if you may also choose not to bet at all on any particular card?

Hint

Let Mn be the fraction of red cards among those not yet seen. Check that Mn is a martingale.

Solution

The next card is red with probability Mn, and E[Mn+1 | Mn] = Mn (the expected fraction does not change as cards are removed at random). By optional stopping, E[MT] = M0 = 1/2 for every stopping rule T, so the best win probability is exactly 1/2. (A simulation of the natural rule "stop when red cards outnumber black" gives 0.499.) If you are allowed to skip bets you still cannot get an edge, because you can only place one bet at the stopping time and the same argument applies. The intuition is the markets one: information about past draws does not change the unconditional probability of a fair process.

Two bidders have independent values uniform on [0, 1] in a first-price sealed-bid auction. Find the symmetric equilibrium bidding function and the seller's expected revenue.

Solution

Suppose the opponent bids βv′. If you bid b, you win when v′ < b/β, with probability b/β, earning (v − b)(b/β). The derivative in b vanishes at b = v/2, so β = 1/2 is self-consistent. 8Market making, microstructure and estimation

Quote a two-sided market on an uncertain quantity, size the spread to your information and to who is trading against you, read a limit order book, update on trades, and give calibrated estimates.

Time 5 hoursPrerequisites Modules 3, 4, 7Feeds interviews at every shop on the list

What a market maker actually does

You are not predicting the future. You are selling immediacy. Someone wants to buy right now; you stand ready to sell to them at a price slightly above what you think the thing is worth, and to buy from the next person at a price slightly below. The gap is your pay. The risk is that you are wrong about what the thing is worth, and that the people who trade with you are disproportionately the ones who are right.

Vocabulary. Fair value (theo): your expected value of the contract. Bid: the price you will pay. Ask (offer): the price you will sell at. Spread: ask minus bid. Edge: how far a trade is in your favour relative to fair value. Someone who sells to your bid hits you; someone who buys at your ask lifts you. Inventory: the position you are left holding. Adverse selection: the tendency for the trades you get to be the ones you would rather not have.

Two kinds of counterparty exist. Uninformed flow trades for reasons unrelated to the contract's value (a fund rebalancing, a hedger). On average you earn your edge from them. Informed flow trades because they know something. On average you lose to them, and the better they are informed, the more you lose per trade. Your spread has to earn more from the first group than it pays to the second.

The quoting recipe

  1. Fair value. Compute the expectation of the contract, not a guess.
  2. Uncertainty. Compute the standard deviation. Variance adds for independent pieces, so it is easy for sums of dice and cards.
  3. Who is on the other side? The more likely a counterparty knows something (it is an interviewer who just peeked at a card, a fast trader, a market that has just moved), the wider you quote.
  4. Width. Wider when you are more uncertain, when the counterparty is more likely informed, or when you do not want inventory. Tighter when competitors are tight and the flow is mostly uninformed.
  5. Skew with inventory. If you are long, lower both quotes slightly: you want to sell, so make your ask more attractive and your bid less. If short, raise both.
  6. After every trade, update. A trade is information, and so is the absence of trades.
In an interview you will always be asked to make a market, even if you feel you have no idea. The worst answer is "I can't". The best is a number, a width, and a reason. "I'd say 14, width two, because the sd is about 3.4 and I'd expect you might have peeked."

Updating on a trade: the number that surprises everyone

The contract settles at the sum of four fair dice (fair value 14). A counterparty who has peeked at two of the dice values the contract at (their two dice) + 7. Suppose such a counterparty buys from you at 14.5. When would they do that? Only if their value exceeds 14.5, meaning their two dice sum to 8 or more (15 of 36 outcomes). Given that, the average of their two dice is 9.33 and their value is 9.33 + 7 = 16.33. So the contract is worth 16.33 in expectation given that they bought, not 14.5 and not 14. You sold at 14.5 and lost 1.83 on average. Conditioning on "someone chose to trade with me" is exactly what the winner's-curse problem in Module 7 was about.

The same logic in a one-line version: you are asked for a market on the sum of two dice (fair 7). A counterparty who has seen one die buys at 8. They only do that if their die is 5 or 6, so the sum averages 5.5 + 3.5 = 9. You lose $1 per trade on average. If you suspect the counterparty knows something, widen until the loss on informed trades is covered by the profit on the others.

The market-making game

Twelve rounds. Each round the contract settles at the sum of four dice (fair value 14). You post a bid and an ask. One counterparty arrives: about 4 in 10 have peeked at two of the four dice and value the contract at their two dice plus 7; the rest have not peeked and hold a noisy opinion centred on 14 with a standard deviation of 2. A counterparty buys at your ask if they value the contract above it, sells at your bid if they value it below, and otherwise walks away. Your realised PnL is noisy. The dashed line (expected PnL of the quotes you chose) measures your actual skill. The curve above it shows the whole trade-off for symmetric quotes.

Reading a limit order book

Most electronic markets run on a limit order book: a list of resting buy orders (bids) and sell orders (asks), each with a price and a size.

  • A limit order names a price. If it can trade immediately against the other side it does; otherwise the unfilled part rests in the book.
  • A market order takes whatever is there, best price first, until it is filled. You pay the spread and may walk through several levels.
  • Price-time priority. The best price trades first; among equal prices, the order that arrived first trades first. Being early in the queue at a price is worth money.
  • Slippage is the difference between the price you expected (usually the best quote or mid) and the average price you got.
  • Resting orders are a free option to everyone else. If news arrives, informed traders hit your stale quote before you can cancel. That is adverse selection in its natural habitat, and it is why fast infrastructure matters.

Estimation and calibration

Estimation questions (Fermi problems, or contests where you give a range and win by being right) test whether you can decompose, bound and stay calibrated. Three rules:

  1. Decompose into pieces you can bound. Number of X = (people) × (fraction who use X) × (uses per person). Bound each piece to within a factor of 2 or 3.
  2. Errors multiply, so work in logs. If each of three factors is uncertain by a factor of 2 (one standard deviation in log space is ln 2 = 0.69) and the errors are independent, the product has log-sd √3 × 0.69 = 1.2, a factor of 3.3. A 90% range is then ×/÷ e1.645 × 1.2 = 7.2. Every extra factor makes your honest range wider, which is why long chains of reasoning feel confident and are not.
  3. Calibrate. A 90% range should miss one time in ten. Most people's ranges miss four or five times in ten. The only fix is practice with feedback.

Inventory, risk and what the numbers look like

A market maker earns a small edge on a huge number of trades, so the edge-to-noise ratio of the strategy is enormous. If you earn 0.5 ticks of edge on each of 1,000 trades a day and each trade has a standard deviation of 3 ticks, your daily mean is 500 ticks and your daily standard deviation is 3√1000 ≈ 95, so the daily Sharpe ratio is about 5.3 and the annualised one is 5.3 × √252 ≈ 84. Real numbers are lower (correlated moves, costs, inventory), but the order of magnitude explains why these firms exist and why they are obsessed with capacity: the strategy cannot be scaled because there are only so many uninformed trades to capture.

  • Hedge. Offset the unwanted exposure in a correlated instrument. Options market makers hedge the underlying (delta hedging, Module 14).
  • Size. Quote small when you are unsure, and bigger when you are confident. The Kelly logic from Module 7 applies to every trade.
  • Do not average down without new information. A position that has lost money is not more attractive because it has lost money.

9Statistics, regression and inference

Estimate, test and regress without fooling yourself: read a t-statistic, understand regression to the mean, see how many strategies it takes to find a fake winner, and use the time-series facts that trading questions rely on.

Time 6 hoursPrerequisites Modules 4 and 6Feeds quant-research tracks, Module 14

Summary statistics you must be able to do in your head

  • Sample mean and standard error. The mean of n independent observations with standard deviation σ has standard error σ/√n. To halve the error you need four times the data. This is the square-root law and it governs how long you must watch a strategy.
  • Sample variance. Divide by n − 1, not n, to be unbiased. (One degree of freedom was spent on estimating the mean.)
  • Correlation. ρ = Cov(X, Y)/(σXσY) lies in [−1, 1]. It measures linear association only. X and X2 for symmetric X are uncorrelated yet perfectly dependent.
  • Fat tails. Returns are not normal. Extreme moves are far more frequent than the bell curve suggests. Use medians and robust measures when outliers are possible, and never trust a 4-sigma probability from a normal table.

Regression

Ordinary least squares chooses the line that minimises the sum of squared vertical residuals. For one predictor:

Simple regression. Slope β = Cov(x, y)/Var(x) = ρσy/σx. Intercept α = y − βx. The fraction of the variance of y explained is R2 = ρ2. Small example: points (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 5), (4, 4) give Sxy = 4, Sxx = 5, so β = 0.8, α = 3.5 − 0.8 × 2.5 = 1.5, and R2 = 42/(5 × 5) = 0.64.

With several predictors, β̂ = (XTX)−1XTy: the fitted values are the orthogonal projection of y onto the span of the columns of X. Each coefficient measures the effect of that variable holding the others fixed (formally, the slope of y on the part of xj not explained by the others; this is the Frisch–Waugh theorem). Things that break regressions: leaving out a variable correlated with both y and a predictor (omitted-variable bias), predictors that are nearly collinear (huge standard errors, unstable signs), and errors whose size depends on the predictor (heteroskedasticity, which fools the usual standard errors).

The t-statistic. t = β̂/SE(β̂). As a rule of thumb |t| > 2 is about 5% significance. For a sample correlation r from n pairs, t = r√(n − 2)/√(1 − r2).

Regression to the mean

If two measurements of something (a trader's results in two years, a fund's returns in two quarters, a test and a retest) have correlation ρ < 1, then an extreme first observation predicts a less extreme second one: the expected standardised value of the second is ρ times the first. With ρ = 0.5, a result two standard deviations above average predicts one standard deviation above average next time. Nothing caused the decline; the first result simply contained luck that does not repeat. Mistaking this for an effect is behind half of the bad narratives in finance ("the manager lost his touch").

Estimation

  • Maximum likelihood. Pick the parameters that make the observed data most probable. For Bernoulli data, p̂ = (successes)/n. For normal data, the sample mean and the variance (divided by n).
  • Bias and variance. The mean squared error of an estimator is bias2 + variance. A slightly biased estimator with much lower variance can win. This is the root of regularisation.
  • Combining estimates. Two independent unbiased estimates with variances σ12 and σ22 combine best with weights proportional to 1/σ2, and the combined variance is 1/(1/σ12 + 1/σ22), always smaller than either.

Hypothesis tests, p-values and the multiple-testing trap

A p-value is the probability, if the null hypothesis were true, of seeing data at least as extreme as what you saw. It is not the probability that the null is true, and not the probability that your result will replicate. A 5% test of a true null still rejects 5% of the time. Run 20 independent tests of nulls that are all true and the chance of at least one false positive is 1 − 0.9520 = 64%. The Bonferroni fix tests each at 0.05/20 = 0.25%. Softer fixes control the false discovery rate.

This is the central statistical sin of quantitative finance: data snooping. Test enough signals, parameters or filters on the same history and the best one will look brilliant by chance. How brilliant? The expected maximum of K independent standard normal estimates is about 1.5 for K = 10, 2.5 for 100, and 3.2 for 1,000. Multiply by the standard error of a Sharpe ratio estimate, which for T years of daily data is about 1/√T (annualised). One year of history and a thousand tries yields an in-sample Sharpe near 3.2 from pure noise.

Sharpe and t-statistics. With daily mean m and standard deviation s, the annualised Sharpe ratio is (m/s)√252. Over Y years, the t-statistic of the mean return is about SR × √Y. A strategy with Sharpe 0.8 needs Y = (2/0.8)2 = 6.3 years before its t-statistic reaches 2. A Sharpe of 2 needs one year.

Bayesian updating

Treat the unknown success probability p as random with a Beta(a, b) prior. After k successes and n − k failures, the posterior is Beta(a + k, b + n − k) and the posterior mean is (a + k)/(a + b + n), a weighted average of the prior mean and the observed frequency, with weights a + b and n. That is shrinkage. With the uniform prior Beta(1, 1) this is Laplace's rule of succession, (k + 1)/(n + 2). Use it when a trader has a 7-for-10 hit rate and you must say what to expect: not 70%.

Time series facts

  • AR(1). xt = φxt−1 + εt, with |φ| < 1 for stationarity. Stationary variance σε2/(1 − φ2); autocorrelation at lag k is φk. The half-life of a shock is ln 2/(−ln φ): for φ = 0.9, 6.6 periods; φ = 0.95, 13.5; φ = 0.99, 69.
  • Continuous time. The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process dX = −κ(X − μ)dt + σdW is the continuous cousin, with half-life ln 2/κ and φ = e−κΔt. A mean-reversion trade is only as good as its half-life relative to your horizon and costs.
  • Random walk. xt = xt−1 + εt has variance growing linearly: sd after t steps is σ√t. Regressing one random walk on another gives a high R2 and a huge t-statistic with no relationship at all (spurious regression). Difference your data or test for cointegration before trusting levels.
  • Volatility conversions. Annual vol = daily vol × √252 ≈ daily × 16. So a 24% annual-vol stock moves about 1.5% on a typical day. Monthly = annual/√12 ≈ annual/3.5.

Machine learning in one page

  • Bias–variance trade-off. Flexible models fit training data closely and noise too; simple models miss structure. Prediction error on new data is minimised in between.
  • Regularisation. Ridge regression, β̂ = (XTX + λI)−1XTy, shrinks coefficients toward zero and stabilises collinear predictors. Lasso (an absolute-value penalty) shrinks some to exactly zero.
  • Validation. Never evaluate on the data you fit. For time series use walk-forward testing: train on the past, test on the future, roll forward. Random shuffling leaks the future into the past.
  • PCA. The principal components are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix; the eigenvalues are the variances along them. The first few often capture most of the variation in a cross-section of returns (a market factor, a size factor, ...).
The backtest sins. Look-ahead: using data that was not known at the time. Survivorship: testing only on companies that still exist. Selection: reporting the best of many tries. Ignoring costs: bid-ask, fees, and the market impact of your own size. Overlap: counting overlapping observations as independent. The rule of thumb: if a backtest looks too good, assume one of these is why.

Problems

Two variables have correlation 0.5, with standard deviations 2 (X) and 3 (Y). What is the slope when regressing Y on X, and what fraction of the variance of Y is explained?

Solution

β = ρσY/σX = 0.5 × 3/2 = 0.75. R2 = ρ2 = 0.25. Regressing X on Y would instead give slope 0.5 × 2/3 = 1/3: the two regressions are not inverses of each other unless |ρ| = 1.

Fit a line by least squares to the points (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 5), (4, 4). What are the slope, intercept and R2? What is the fitted value at x = 5?

Solution

Means 2.5 and 3.5. Sxy = (−1.5)(−1.5) + (−0.5)(−0.5) + (0.5)(1.5) + (1.5)(0.5) = 4, Sxx = 5, Syy = 5. Slope 0.8, intercept 3.5 − 2 = 1.5, R2 = 16/25 = 0.64. At x = 5: 1.5 + 4 = 5.5. (Extrapolating beyond the data is where regressions earn their reputation for failing.)

Let X ~ N(0, 1) and Y = X2. What is Cov(X, Y)? Are X and Y independent?

Solution

Cov = E[X3] − E[X]E[X2] = 0 − 0 = 0, since odd moments of a symmetric distribution vanish. They are certainly not independent: knowing |X| = √Y tells you almost everything. Zero correlation rules out only a linear relationship.

A strategy earns a daily mean of 0.05% with a daily standard deviation of 1%. What is its annualised Sharpe ratio (252 days), and how many years of data would you need to reach a t-statistic of 2?

Solution

Daily Sharpe 0.05, annualised 0.05 × √252 = 0.79. The t-statistic after Y years is SR√Y, so reaching 2 needs Y = (2/0.794)2 ≈ 6.3 years. Reassuringly, real Sharpe-0.8 strategies exist; the sobering part is how long you must watch before you can prove it, and how few parameter choices you can afford to try along the way.

Two independent unbiased estimates of a quantity have variances 4 and 1. What weights minimise the variance of a weighted average, and what is the resulting variance?

Solution

Weights proportional to the precisions 1/4 and 1: normalised, 0.2 and 0.8. Variance = 1/(1/4 + 1) = 0.8, below the better estimate's 1. Check: 0.22(4) + 0.82(1) = 0.16 + 0.64 = 0.8.

Two series have a sample correlation of 0.3 over 100 observations. What is the t-statistic, and is it significant at the 5% level?

Solution

t = 0.3√98/√(1 − 0.09) = 0.3 × 9.90/0.954 = 3.11. With 98 degrees of freedom the two-sided p-value is about 0.002, so yes, significant. But remember: significant is not important. An R2 of 9% means 91% of the variance is unexplained.

You test 20 independent signals at the 5% level. If none has any real predictive power, what is the chance at least one appears significant? What per-test threshold keeps the family-wise error at 5%?

Solution

1 − 0.9520 = 64%. Bonferroni: 0.05/20 = 0.0025 per test (the exact value 1 − 0.951/20 = 0.00256 is almost the same). Expected number of false positives is 20 × 0.05 = 1.

A coin has a Beta(2, 2) prior on its heads probability. You flip it 10 times and see 7 heads. What is the posterior distribution and mean? What would the maximum-likelihood estimate and the Laplace estimate be?

Solution

Posterior Beta(2 + 7, 2 + 3) = Beta(9, 5), mean 9/14 = 0.643. MLE: 7/10 = 0.70. Laplace's rule (uniform prior): 8/12 = 0.667. The stronger your prior, the more the estimate is pulled toward 1/2. The posterior mean is the weighted average (4 × 0.5 + 10 × 0.7)/14.

A spread follows an AR(1) with φ = 0.9 and innovation standard deviation σ. What is its half-life, and how does its stationary standard deviation compare with σ?

Solution

Half-life = ln 2/−ln 0.9 = 0.693/0.1054 = 6.6 periods. Stationary standard deviation = σ/√(1 − 0.81) = σ/0.436 = 2.29σ. So the spread typically sits more than two innovation sizes from its mean, and a deviation takes about 7 periods to halve: a trade that costs more than the reversion it earns in that time is a loser.

A researcher tries 100 parameter combinations on 4 years of daily data and finds a best in-sample annualised Sharpe of 1.5. What Sharpe would you expect from the best of 100 worthless strategies, and how impressed should you be?

Hint

The standard error of an annualised Sharpe estimated from T years of data is about 1/√T. The expected maximum of 100 standard normals is about 2.5.

Solution

Standard error = 1/√4 = 0.5. Expected best of 100 pure-noise strategies ≈ 2.5 × 0.5 = 1.25. The observed 1.5 is only half a standard error above what noise alone produces, so you should be unimpressed. To believe it you need either far fewer trials, a longer history, an economic reason decided before looking, or a clean out-of-sample test the researcher could not tune against.

Series and sums

The five you will use. Geometric: ∑k=0∞ rk = 1/(1 − r) for |r| < 1. Differentiating it gives ∑ k rk = r/(1 − r)2, and once more ∑ k2rk = r(1 + r)/(1 − r)3. With r = 1/2 those are 2 and 6. Optimisation

  • One variable: set the derivative to zero, check the second derivative (positive: minimum, negative: maximum). On a closed interval also compare the endpoints.
  • Constraints: either substitute the constraint, or use a Lagrange multiplier: at a constrained optimum of f subject to g = 0, ∇f = λ∇g. Maximise xy subject to x + 2y = 12: ∇(xy) = (y, x) = λ(1, 2), so x = 2y, hence y = 3, x = 6, and xy = 18.

Integrals worth knowing cold

The Gamma family. Γ(n) = ∫0∞ xn−1e−xdx = (n − 1)!, so ∫0∞ x2e−xdx = 2. Also Γ(1/2) = √π. Gaussian: ∫−∞∞ e−x2/2dx = √(2π). Beta: ∫01 xa(1 − x)bdx = a!b!/(a + b + 1)!. So ∫01 x2(1 − x)dx = 2!1!/4! = 1/12.

Two techniques: integration by parts (∫u dv = uv − ∫v du), which produces the Gamma recursion; and differentiating under the integral sign: if you know I(a) = ∫ e−axdx = 1/a, then −I′(a) = ∫ xe−axdx = 1/a2, and so on. That is how expectations of exponential variables fall out in two lines.

Linear algebra as geometry

A 2×2 matrix is a machine that deforms the plane: it sends the unit circle to an ellipse and the unit square to a parallelogram. Four numbers summarise its behaviour:

  • Determinant = the factor by which areas scale (negative if orientation flips). Zero means the plane collapses onto a line (the matrix is not invertible).
  • Trace = sum of eigenvalues. Determinant = product of eigenvalues.
  • Eigenvectors are the directions the matrix only stretches: Av = λv. For a 2×2: λ = tr/2 ± √(tr2/4 − det).
  • Singular values are the semi-axes of the ellipse the unit circle becomes; they are the square roots of the eigenvalues of ATA.

Facts for quant questions

  • Symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors (the spectral theorem). Covariance matrices are symmetric and positive semi-definite: all eigenvalues ≥ 0, equivalently wTΣw ≥ 0 for every portfolio w, since that quantity is the portfolio variance.
  • Diagonalisation turns powers into easy arithmetic: A = PDP−1 gives An = PDnP−1. The Fibonacci recurrence is the matrix [[1,1],[1,0]] raised to a power, with eigenvalues the golden ratio and its negative reciprocal.
  • Markov chains. The stationary distribution is the eigenvector of P (acting on row vectors) for eigenvalue 1. Pick λ = 1 and normalise. For the weather chain it is (5/6, 1/6); the other eigenvalue (0.4) controls how fast the chain forgets its start.
  • Rank = number of independent rows. If rows are in arithmetic progression (like 1,2,3 / 4,5,6 / 7,8,9) the determinant is 0 and the rank is 2.
  • PCA finds the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix. The largest eigenvalue's eigenvector is the direction of greatest variance.

Three assets have the same pairwise correlation ρ. What values of ρ are possible? Could all three pairwise correlations equal −0.8?

The correlation matrix has eigenvalues 1 + 2ρ (eigenvector (1,1,1)) and 1 − ρ (twice). Positive semi-definiteness needs 1 + 2ρ ≥ 0 and 1 − ρ ≥ 0, so −1/2 ≤ ρ ≤ 1. At −0.8 an eigenvalue is 1 + 2(−0.8) = −0.6 < 0: impossible. Intuition: three assets cannot all be strongly negatively related to each other, since if two move opposite to the third, they move together. With n assets the lower bound is −1/(n − 1).

Small cases and the Josephus pattern

People 1 to n stand in a circle. Starting with person 1, you eliminate every second person (2, 4, 6, ...), continuing around the circle until one remains. Who survives? Compute n = 1 to 8 by hand: survivors 1, 1, 3, 1, 3, 5, 7, 1. The survivor resets to 1 at every power of two and then climbs by 2. So write n = 2m + L with 0 ≤ L < 2m: the survivor is 2L + 1. For n = 41 = 32 + 9 the survivor is 19, and for 100 = 64 + 36 it is 73. The method, not the formula, is the lesson: tabulate small cases until the pattern is obvious, then prove it by induction.

Invariants and parity

The mutilated chessboard. Remove two opposite corners of an 8×8 board. Can the remaining 62 squares be tiled by 31 dominoes? A domino always covers one dark and one light square. Opposite corners have the same colour, so the board now has 32 of one colour and 30 of the other. The tiling is impossible. The invariant is "dark minus light", and every domino leaves it unchanged. Whenever a question asks "is it possible?", ask which quantity every legal move preserves.

Pigeonhole

If you put more than n objects in n boxes, some box holds two. The skill is choosing the boxes. Pick any 51 numbers from 1 to 100; two must be such that one divides the other. Write each number as 2k × (odd part). There are only 50 possible odd parts (1, 3, ..., 99), so two of the 51 numbers share an odd part, and then the smaller divides the larger.

Encoding and information

A test with k possible outcomes can distinguish at most k hypotheses, and t sequential tests at most kt. If you need to identify one of N things, you need kt ≥ N. A weighing on a balance scale has three outcomes, so three weighings can distinguish up to 27 cases; a prisoner who dies or lives gives two outcomes, so 10 prisoners distinguish 210 = 1024 bottles. Then you design the tests so each hypothesis produces a different outcome pattern.

Backward induction

Solve the last decision first, then the second to last, and so on. The pirates: five pirates (A the most senior) must split 100 coins. The most senior proposes a split; all vote; if at least half agree it passes, otherwise the proposer is thrown overboard and the next senior proposes. Pirates are logical, greedy and bloodthirsty. Work backward: with one pirate left he takes all. With two (D, E), D proposes 100 for himself and his own vote carries it. With three (C, D, E), E would get nothing if C died, so C buys E's vote with 1 coin: (99, 0, 1). With four (B, C, D, E), D would get nothing if B died, so B buys D: (99, 0, 1, 0). With five, A needs two more votes and buys the pirates who would get nothing under B's plan, C and E, with one coin each: (98, 0, 1, 0, 1).

Nim. Piles of stones; players alternately remove any number from one pile; whoever takes the last stone wins. The nim-sum is the XOR of the pile sizes. A position is lost for the player to move iff the nim-sum is 0. Piles 3, 4, 5: 3 ⊕ 4 ⊕ 5 = 2, non-zero, so the first player wins by moving to nim-sum 0: reduce the 3-pile to 1 (1 ⊕ 4 ⊕ 5 = 0). The simpler cousin: "remove 1 to 3 stones, last stone wins": the losing positions are the multiples of 4.

Symmetry and the 100 doors

One hundred doors start closed. On pass k you toggle every k-th door. After pass 100, which doors are open? Door n is toggled once for each divisor of n. Divisors pair up (d, n/d) so the count is even, unless n is a perfect square, where one divisor pairs with itself. So the open doors are the perfect squares: 1, 4, 9, ..., 100. That is 10 doors.

Hundred prisoners and the cycle trick

One hundred prisoners are numbered 1 to 100. A room holds 100 boxes, each containing a different prisoner's number in a random permutation. Each prisoner may open 50 boxes and must find their own number; all must succeed or everyone dies. No communication afterwards. Guessing gives (1/2)100. The winning strategy: prisoner i opens box i, reads the number inside, opens that box, and so on, following the cycle. The permutation splits into cycles, and prisoner i is guaranteed to be on the cycle containing number i. Everyone succeeds exactly when no cycle is longer than 50. The probability that a random permutation of 100 has a cycle longer than 50 is ∑k=51100 1/k ≈ 0.688, so they all survive with probability 31.2%. Turning independent hopelessness into a shared event is the whole trick.

Common knowledge: blue eyes

An island has k blue-eyed people, everyone can see everyone else's eyes but not their own, and nobody is allowed to speak about eye colour. Anyone who deduces their own eye colour must leave at midnight. A visitor announces "I see at least one person with blue eyes." Now what? If k = 1, that blue-eyed person sees no blue eyes, concludes it is them, and leaves that night. If k = 2, each sees one blue-eyed person; when nobody leaves on night one, each concludes there must be two, so both leave on night two. By induction, all k blue-eyed people leave on night k. The announcement told everyone nothing they did not know when k ≥ 2, but it created common knowledge, and common knowledge is what starts the clock.

Solution

Five pirates, 100 coins, the rules from the lesson. What does pirate A propose, and why do the others accept?

Many ants walk on a stick of length 100 cm, each at 1 cm/s, some left and some right. When two ants meet they bounce and reverse direction. The stick has open ends and ants walk off. What is the longest time until every ant has left the stick?

Hint

Instead of bouncing, imagine the ants pass through each other and swap labels.

Solution

Bouncing ants are indistinguishable from ants that pass through each other (the set of positions is identical), so each "ghost" ant simply walks to its end. The worst case is an ant at one end heading toward the other: 100 seconds. The collisions, which look so complicated, change nothing about the answer.

How many times in a day do the hour and minute hands of an analogue clock overlap? When is the first overlap after 12:00?

Solution

The minute hand gains one lap on the hour hand every 12/11 hours, so overlaps occur 11 times in 12 hours and 22 times in a day. The first is at 12/11 hours = 1 hour 5.45 minutes, about 1:05:27. (In 12 hours the overlaps are at 12:00, about 1:05, 2:11, and so on up to about 10:55, and then 12:00 again starts the next cycle. That is 11 distinct overlaps per 12 hours, not 12.)

In Nim with piles of 3, 4 and 5 stones, who wins with best play and what is the winning first move? What if the piles were 1, 2, 3?

Solution

3 ⊕ 4 ⊕ 5 = 2, non-zero, so the player to move wins. They need a pile p with p ⊕ 2 < p: the 3-pile works (3 ⊕ 2 = 1), so remove 2 stones from it, leaving 1, 4, 5 with nim-sum 0. For 1, 2, 3: 1 ⊕ 2 ⊕ 3 = 0, so the player to move loses against best play.

Twelve coins look identical, but one is either heavier or lighter than the rest. Using a balance scale three times, find the odd coin and say whether it is heavy or light.

Hint

There are 24 possibilities and three weighings give 27 outcomes. The first weighing should split 4 against 4, leaving 4 unweighed.

Solution

Weighing 1: coins 1–4 against 5–8. If balanced, the odd coin is among 9–12 and coins 1–8 are genuine. Weighing 2: 9, 10, 11 against three genuine coins. If balanced, 12 is odd (weigh it against a genuine coin to see whether heavy or light). If 9–11 is heavy, the odd coin is heavy among them: weigh 9 against 10; the heavier is odd, and if balanced it is 11. (Light is symmetric.) If unbalanced, say 1–4 is heavy. Then either one of 1–4 is heavy or one of 5–8 is light. Weighing 2: {1, 2, 5} against {3, 6, 9} with 9 genuine. Left heavy: the culprit is heavy 1, heavy 2, or light 6; weigh 1 against 2 (heavier is odd, balanced means 6 is light). Balanced: the culprit is heavy 4, light 7, or light 8; weigh 7 against 8 (lighter is odd, balanced means 4 is heavy). Right heavy: the culprit is light 5 or heavy 3; weigh 3 against a genuine coin (heavier means 3 is odd, balanced means 5 is light).

One hundred prisoners stand in a line, each wearing a red or blue hat, each able to see all hats in front of them but not their own or those behind. Starting from the back, each in turn guesses their own hat colour aloud and everyone hears the guesses. What strategy guarantees at least 99 survive?

Solution

Agree that "red" means "I see an even number of red hats". The last prisoner sees 99 hats and announces red if the number of red hats he sees is even, otherwise blue. He survives with probability 1/2, but everyone now knows the parity. The next prisoner counts the reds he sees ahead of him; if the parity matches the announced one, his own hat is blue, otherwise red. Each later prisoner updates the parity using the colours already called behind him (the correct ones) and the hats ahead. So 99 survive for certain. Technique: a single bit of shared information (parity) lets the whole group infer individual states.

Show that among any 5 integers, there are two whose difference is divisible by 4. Then show that among any 51 integers there are two whose sum or difference is a multiple of 100.

Solution

First: there are 4 remainders mod 4 and 5 integers, so two share a remainder, and their difference is divisible by 4. Second: sort residues mod 100 into 51 boxes {0}, {1, 99}, {2, 98}, ..., {49, 51}, {50}. With 51 integers two fall in the same box. If their residues are equal, their difference is a multiple of 100; if they are 100 minus each other, their sum is. Again, the work was in designing the boxes.

A method for finding the rule

Number theory toolkit

Factorisation, divisors, gcd

  • If n = ∏ piei, the number of divisors is ∏(ei + 1) and their sum is ∏(piei+1 − 1)/(pi − 1). For 360 = 23·32·5: 4 × 3 × 2 = 24 divisors, summing to 15 × 13 × 6 = 1170. Only perfect squares have an odd number of divisors.
  • Euclid's algorithm: gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a mod b). lcm = ab/gcd. gcd(1071, 462): 1071 = 2·462 + 147, 462 = 3·147 + 21, 147 = 7·21, so the gcd is 21.
  • Trailing zeros of n! = ⌊n/5⌋ + ⌊n/25⌋ + ⌊n/125⌋ + ··· (count factors of 5; there are always enough 2s). For 100!: 20 + 4 = 24.
  • Linear Diophantine: ax + by = c has integer solutions iff gcd(a, b) divides c.
  • Pythagorean triples come from (m2 − n2, 2mn, m2 + n2): 3-4-5, 5-12-13, 8-15-17, 7-24-25.

Modular arithmetic

  • Residues add, subtract and multiply: (a + b) mod m = ((a mod m) + (b mod m)) mod m, and the same for products and powers.
  • Last digits cycle. Powers of 2, 3, 7, 8 have period 4 in the last digit; 4 and 9 have period 2; 0, 1, 5, 6 are fixed. 71, 72, 73, 74 end in 7, 9, 3, 1.
  • Fermat's little theorem: if p is prime and p ∤ a, then ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p). Euler: aφ(m) ≡ 1 (mod m) when gcd(a, m) = 1, with φ(m) = m∏(1 − 1/p) over the primes dividing m.
  • Modular inverse of a mod m exists iff gcd = 1 and is found by extended Euclid. 7 × 15 = 105 = 4·26 + 1, so 7−1 ≡ 15 (mod 26).
  • Chinese remainder theorem: congruences to coprime moduli combine into one modulo the product. x ≡ 2 (3), 3 (5), 2 (7) gives x ≡ 23 (mod 105).
  • Squares mod small numbers: mod 3 are {0, 1}; mod 4 are {0, 1}; mod 8 are {0, 1, 4}. So no square is 2 mod 3, no sum of two squares is 3 mod 4, and n2 + 1 is never divisible by 3.
  • Digit sums. A number is congruent to its digit sum mod 9 and mod 3; the digital root is the residue mod 9 (with 9 for multiples of 9).

Bases

2025 = 1024 + 512 + 256 + 128 + 64 + 32 + 8 + 1, so in binary it is 11111101001 (11 bits, eight 1s). A number has ⌊log2 n⌋ + 1 bits. Converting to base b is repeated division by b reading remainders backwards.

Solution

Show that n2 + 1 is never divisible by 3, and that no integer of the form 4k + 3 is a sum of two squares.

Hash maps and prefix sums

If a question is about contiguous ranges and sums, define the prefix sum Si and note that a range sum is a difference Sj − Si. "How many subarrays sum to k?" becomes "for each prefix, how many earlier prefixes equal Sj − k?", which a hash map answers in O(1). Total O(n).

Python: count subarrays with sum k
from collections import defaultdict

def count_subarrays_sum_k(a, k):
    seen = defaultdict(int)
    seen[0] = 1                      # the empty prefix
    s = ans = 0
    for x in a:
        s += x
        ans += seen[s - k]           # earlier prefixes that leave exactly k
        seen[s] += 1
    return ans

Sliding window and the monotonic deque

For fixed-size windows, add the new element and drop the old one. For a window maximum keep a deque of indices whose values decrease: an element behind a larger one can never be the maximum again.

Python: sliding window maximum, O(n)
from collections import deque

def window_max(a, k):
    dq, out = deque(), []            # dq holds indices; their values decrease
    for i, x in enumerate(a):
        while dq and a[dq[-1]] <= x:
            dq.pop()                 # dominated elements can never be a max
        dq.append(i)
        if dq[0] <= i - k:
            dq.popleft()             # left the window
        if i >= k - 1:
            out.append(a[dq[0]])
    return out

Two heaps: the running median

Keep the lower half in a max-heap and the upper half in a min-heap, balanced to differ by at most one in size. The median is the top of the larger heap or the mean of the two tops. Each insert is O(log n).

Python: streaming median
import heapq

class RunningMedian:
    def __init__(self):
        self.lo, self.hi = [], []    # lo: max-heap (stored negated), hi: min-heap

    def add(self, x):
        heapq.heappush(self.lo, -x)
        heapq.heappush(self.hi, -heapq.heappop(self.lo))
        if len(self.hi) > len(self.lo):
            heapq.heappush(self.lo, -heapq.heappop(self.hi))

    def median(self):
        if len(self.lo) > len(self.hi):
            return float(-self.lo[0])
        return (-self.lo[0] + self.hi[0]) / 2

Monotonic stack

"Next greater element", "largest rectangle in a histogram", "stock span": keep a stack of indices with monotone values and resolve entries as they are popped. Every element is pushed and popped once, so O(n).

Python: next greater element
def next_greater(a):
    res, st = [-1] * len(a), []      # st: indices whose next greater is still unknown
    for i, x in enumerate(a):
        while st and a[st[-1]] < x:
            res[st.pop()] = x
        st.append(i)
    return res

Binary search on the answer

If you can test "is value x feasible?" in O(n), and feasibility is monotone in x, binary search over x. Example: split an array into m parts so the largest part sum is as small as possible. Feasibility check: greedily pack parts up to a cap.

Python: split array, minimise the largest sum
def split_array(a, m):
    """Split a into at most m contiguous parts minimising the largest part sum."""
    def parts_needed(cap):
        n, cur = 1, 0
        for x in a:
            if cur + x > cap:
                n, cur = n + 1, 0
            cur += x
        return n
    lo, hi = max(a), sum(a)          # the answer lies in [lo, hi]
    while lo < hi:
        mid = (lo + hi) // 2
        if parts_needed(mid) <= m:
            hi = mid                 # feasible: try smaller
        else:
            lo = mid + 1
    return lo

def min_coins(coins, amount):
    INF = float('inf')
    dp = [0] + [INF] * amount
    for a in range(1, amount + 1):
        dp[a] = min((dp[a - c] + 1 for c in coins if c <= a), default=INF)

def ways(coins, amount):

    for c in coins:                  # coins in the OUTER loop counts combinations
        for a in range(c, amount + 1):
            dp[a] += dp[a - c]
    return dp[amount]

def lis_length(a):

Python: edit distance
def edit_distance(s, t):
    prev = list(range(len(t) + 1))
    for i, a in enumerate(s, 1):
        cur = [i]
        for j, b in enumerate(t, 1):
            cur.append(min(prev[j] + 1,              # delete
                           cur[j - 1] + 1,           # insert
                           prev[j - 1] + (a != b)))  # substitute or match
        prev = cur
    return prev[-1]

Python: Dijkstra with a heap
import heapq

def dijkstra(graph, src):            # graph: {u: [(v, weight), ...]}
    dist = {src: 0}
    pq = [(0, src)]
    while pq:
        d, u = heapq.heappop(pq)
        if d > dist.get(u, float('inf')):
            continue                 # stale entry
        for v, w in graph.get(u, ()):
            nd = d + w
            if nd < dist.get(v, float('inf')):
                dist[v] = nd
                heapq.heappush(pq, (nd, v))
    return dist
Python: union-find (disjoint set union)
class DSU:
    def __init__(self, n):
        self.p = list(range(n))
        self.size = [1] * n

    def find(self, x):
        while self.p[x] != x:
            self.p[x] = self.p[self.p[x]]    # path halving
            x = self.p[x]
        return x

    def union(self, a, b):
        a, b = self.find(a), self.find(b)
        if a == b:
            return False
        if self.size[a] < self.size[b]:
            a, b = b, a
        self.p[b] = a
        self.size[a] += self.size[b]
        return True

Bits and math

XOR of a list where each value appears twice leaves the single unmatched one. x & (x - 1) clears the lowest set bit, so counting bits takes as many steps as there are ones. Bitmasks enumerate subsets. Square-and-multiply computes powers in O(log e), and the same idea powers the matrix-Fibonacci trick from Module 10.

Python: XOR trick, popcount, subsets, modular power
def single_number(a):                # every other value appears exactly twice
    r = 0
    for x in a:
        r ^= x
    return r

def popcount(x):
    c = 0
    while x:
        x &= x - 1                   # clears the lowest set bit
        c += 1
    return c

def subsets(a):                      # all 2^n subsets via bitmasks
    n = len(a)
    for mask in range(1 << n):
        yield [a[i] for i in range(n) if mask >> i & 1]

def power_mod(b, e, m):              # square and multiply, O(log e)
    r, b = 1, b % m
    while e:
        if e & 1:
            r = r * b % m
        b = b * b % m
        e >>= 1
    return r

Randomness and Monte Carlo

  • Fisher–Yates shuffle produces every permutation with equal probability. The classic bug is swapping each element with a random position over the whole array, which makes some permutations likelier than others.
  • Reservoir sampling picks k items uniformly from a stream of unknown length: keep the first k; for item i (0-indexed) keep it with probability k/(i + 1), replacing a random reservoir slot.
  • Rejection sampling turns one generator into another: to build rand7 from rand5, make a uniform number on 0 to 24 as 5(a − 1) + (b − 1), reject 21 to 24, and take the rest mod 7.
  • Weighted choice: build cumulative weights and binary-search a uniform draw scaled by the total.
  • Monte Carlo error falls like 1/√N: four times the samples for half the error. Estimating π from the fraction of random points inside a quarter circle has a standard deviation of 4√(p(1 − p)) ≈ 1.64 per sample.
Python: reservoir sampling, Fisher–Yates, rand7
import random

def reservoir(stream, k):
    res = []
    for i, x in enumerate(stream):
        if i < k:
            res.append(x)
        else:
            j = random.randint(0, i)         # keep x with probability k/(i+1)
            if j < k:
                res[j] = x
    return res

def shuffle(a):                              # Fisher-Yates: uniform over all n! orders
    for i in range(len(a) - 1, 0, -1):
        j = random.randint(0, i)
        a[i], a[j] = a[j], a[i]

def rand7(rand5):                            # rand5() returns 1..5 uniformly
    while True:
        v = 5 * (rand5() - 1) + (rand5() - 1)    # uniform on 0..24
        if v < 21:                           # reject 21..24 to stay uniform
            return v % 7 + 1

import bisect, itertools, random

def weighted_choice(items, weights):
    cum = list(itertools.accumulate(weights))            # running totals
    return items[bisect.bisect(cum, random.random() * cum[-1])]

def estimate_pi(n, seed=0):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    hits = sum(rng.random() ** 2 + rng.random() ** 2 < 1 for _ in range(n))
    p = hits / n
    return 4 * p, 4 * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n)         # estimate and its standard error

Quant favourites

The order book shows up in dev and quant interviews at market makers. The standard design: two heaps, bids keyed by negative price so the best (highest) is on top, asks keyed by price, each entry with an arrival counter for time priority. An incoming order matches while it crosses the best opposing price; the rest rests in its heap. A partially filled resting order keeps its original arrival time and queue position.

Python: limit-order matching with price-time priority
import heapq

class Book:
    """Price-time priority matching. Heaps hold (price key, arrival time, qty)."""
    def __init__(self):
        self.bids, self.asks, self.t = [], [], 0     # bids store -price

    def add(self, side, price, qty, rest=True):
        self.t += 1
        fills = []
        if side == 'B':
            while qty and self.asks and self.asks[0][0] <= price:
                p, t, q = heapq.heappop(self.asks)
                take = min(qty, q)
                fills.append((p, take))
                qty -= take
                if q > take:
                    heapq.heappush(self.asks, (p, t, q - take))   # keeps its place in the queue
            if qty and rest:
                heapq.heappush(self.bids, (-price, self.t, qty))
        else:
            while qty and self.bids and -self.bids[0][0] >= price:
                p, t, q = heapq.heappop(self.bids)
                take = min(qty, q)
                fills.append((-p, take))
                qty -= take
                if q > take:
                    heapq.heappush(self.bids, (p, t, q - take))
            if qty and rest:
                heapq.heappush(self.asks, (price, self.t, qty))
        return fills

    def best(self):
        return (-self.bids[0][0] if self.bids else None,
                self.asks[0][0] if self.asks else None)

Rolling statistics. Naive variance (sum of squares minus square of the sum) loses precision. Welford's one-pass update is stable and O(1) per observation. Moving averages use a running sum with a deque. Max drawdown is a running peak and the worst fall from it (see Kadane above).

Python: Welford's online mean and variance
class Welford:
    """Online mean and variance in one pass, numerically stable."""
    def __init__(self):
        self.n, self.mean, self.m2 = 0, 0.0, 0.0

    def add(self, x):
        self.n += 1
        d = x - self.mean
        self.mean += d / self.n
        self.m2 += d * (x - self.mean)

    @property
    def var(self):
        return self.m2 / (self.n - 1) if self.n > 1 else 0.0

Stock trading with limited transactions. A famous family: one buy-sell; unlimited; at most k; with a cooldown or fees. The state machine view (holding or flat, after j transactions) makes all of them easy. With k large the problem collapses to adding up every upward step.

Python: best profit with at most k transactions
def max_profit(prices, k):
    n = len(prices)
    if n < 2 or k == 0:
        return 0
    if k >= n // 2:                              # effectively unlimited trades
        return sum(max(0, b - a) for a, b in zip(prices, prices[1:]))
    hold = [float('-inf')] * (k + 1)             # best cash while holding, after j buys
    cash = [0] * (k + 1)                         # best cash while flat, after j sells
    for p in prices:
        for j in range(1, k + 1):
            hold[j] = max(hold[j], cash[j - 1] - p)
            cash[j] = max(cash[j], hold[j] + p)
    return cash[k]

Why is summing a 2D array row by row faster than column by column?Memory is laid out row by row and the CPU fetches 64-byte cache lines. Row order reads neighbouring bytes (cache hits); column order jumps a whole row each step (a miss almost every time). Same arithmetic, many times slower.

What happens when a signed int overflows in C++?Undefined behaviour: the compiler may assume it never happens and optimise accordingly. Use a wider type (long long, __int128), unsigned arithmetic where wrap-around is intended, or check bounds first.

Solution

The answer is 4 (for example 2, 3, 7, 18). Keep tails[i] = the smallest possible last element of an increasing subsequence of length i + 1. Payoffs and what an option is

A call gives the right to buy at the strike K on or before expiry; a put the right to sell. At expiry a call pays max(S − K, 0) and a put max(K − S, 0). Before expiry, an option's price is intrinsic value (what it would pay if exercised now) plus time value (the chance it becomes worth more). Time value is largest at the money and shrinks to zero at expiry.

Options are convex in the underlying: a long option's payoff curves upward. By Jensen's inequality the average payoff over a spread of outcomes exceeds the payoff at the average outcome, so uncertainty itself has value, which is why option prices rise with volatility. Build the strategies below and notice how every named structure is just a recipe of calls, puts and stock.

Strategy builder: profit at expiry versus today

Forwards and put–call parity

A forward contract to buy at K at time T has no optionality, and to hold stock until T costs interest, so the fair forward price is F = SerT (less any dividends). Now compare two portfolios at expiry: long a call and short a put with the same strike, versus a forward. If ST > K the call pays ST − K and the put pays nothing; if ST < K you lose K − ST on the short put. Either way you net ST − K. Same payoffs, so the same price today:

Put–call parity. C − P = S − Ke−rT (European options, no dividends). With dividends, subtract their present value from S. At zero rates this is just C − P = S − K.

If parity is violated you can lock in free money: sell the expensive side, buy the cheap side, hedge with stock and borrowing. The standard trades are the conversion (long stock + long put + short call, which has a fixed payoff of K) and the reversal (its mirror). A box spread (a bull call spread plus a bear put spread over the same two strikes) is worth exactly (K2 − K1)e−rT regardless of the stock: it is a zero-coupon bond.

Pricing by replication: the binomial model

Suppose the stock moves in one step to uS or dS, and cash earns 1 + r. You can build a portfolio of Δ shares and a cash amount B that matches the option's payoff in both states, so the option must cost what the portfolio costs. Solving two equations gives Δ = (Vu − Vd)/(S(u − d)). The tidy way to price: define the risk-neutral probability

q = (1 + r − d)/(u − d), and V = [q Vu + (1 − q)Vd]/(1 + r).

The real-world probability of an up move does not appear anywhere. Pricing by no-arbitrage does not depend on beliefs about the drift: the option is priced as if the stock earned the risk-free rate. For American options compare at every node the continuation value with the immediate exercise value and keep the larger; this is where early exercise of puts shows up.

Black–Scholes in your head

Let the log-price move with volatility σ. For a European call:

C = S N(d1) − Ke−rT N(d2),  d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r + σ2/2)T]/(σ√T),  d2 = d1 − σ√T

Interpretations worth memorising: N(d2) is the risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money; N(d1) is the call's delta; the price is the discounted expected payoff under the risk-neutral measure. You will rarely compute it in an interview. You will often use these approximations at the money, with small rates:

Back-of-envelope. ATM call ≈ 0.4 × S × σ√T. ATM straddle (call + put) ≈ 0.8 × S × σ√T. ATM delta ≈ 0.5. The constant 0.4 is 1/√(2π) = 0.399, the same number as E[max(Z, 0)]. Check: S = K = 200, σ = 30%, T = 0.25 gives 0.4 × 200 × 0.15 = 12; the exact answer with zero rates is 11.96.

Delta hedging: how an options desk actually makes money

Buy an option and short Δ shares so your net delta is zero. Over a short interval your profit is, to a good approximation,

P&L ≈ ½ Γ (ΔS)2 + Θ Δt,   which at fair value breaks even when the realised move equals the implied move.

You earn gamma profit when the stock moves (in either direction, because Γ > 0), and you pay theta every day for holding the convexity. The two cancel when realised volatility equals the volatility implied in the option's price. If you buy an option at 25% implied volatility and the stock then realises 30%, you are net positive; at 20% you lose. A useful translation: a 25% annual volatility on a $100 stock means a typical daily move of 25/√252 ≈ $1.57 (the same as ≈ 25/16). Trading options is therefore trading the disagreement between implied and realised volatility, and market makers manage their book's Greeks to remain flat in delta and bounded in gamma and vega.

  • Variance adds, volatility does not. Total variance is σ2T. Volatility 30% for three months and then 20% for three months averages √((0.09 + 0.04)/2) = 25.5%, not 25%.
  • Implied volatility is the σ that makes the model match the market price. Implied volatility varies by strike (the smile or skew: equity puts usually trade at higher implied volatility because of crash fear) and by maturity (the term structure).
  • Risk-neutral versus real-world. Option prices embed risk-neutral probabilities (drift = risk-free rate). Do not use them as forecasts of the real-world probability of an outcome.

Futures mechanics

A futures contract is a standardised agreement to buy or sell an asset at a set price on a future date, traded on an exchange and cleared through a clearing house. Four ideas carry most interview questions:

  • Multiplier, tick, tick value. The price moves in minimum increments (ticks). Each point of price is worth a fixed dollar amount (the multiplier). Tick value = tick size × multiplier. P&L = (exit − entry) × multiplier × contracts, with the sign flipped for a short.
  • Margin and leverage. You post a performance bond (initial margin), typically a few percent of the notional, not the full value. Positions are marked to market daily: gains and losses are paid in cash every day, and if your balance falls below the maintenance level you receive a margin call. Leverage = notional/margin, which is why small moves are large P&L.
  • Settlement. Equity-index and short-term-rate futures settle in cash; bond and many commodity futures settle by physical delivery, though almost all positions are closed or rolled before then.
  • Rolling. A contract expires, so to keep exposure you sell the expiring month and buy the next. The price difference between the two (the calendar spread) is a cost or a gain of holding the position.

Cost of carry, basis and the cash-and-carry arbitrage

Why is a future's price what it is? Because you could replicate a long future by buying the asset today and holding it. Buying costs the interest you forgo and pays whatever the asset yields. For a stock index with continuous dividend yield q and interest rate r:

F = S e(r − q)T

For a commodity with storage cost u and a convenience yield y (the benefit of physically holding it): F = S e(r + u − y)T. When F > S the market is in contango; when F < S it is in backwardation (a high convenience yield: the asset is scarce right now). The basis is F − S, and it must shrink to zero by expiry.

The enforcement mechanism is arbitrage. If the future is rich, borrow to buy the asset, sell the future, and deliver at expiry: you earn F − Se(r−q)T with no risk. If it is cheap, do the reverse. With S = 5,000, r = 5%, q = 1.5% and T = 3 months, F = 5,043.94. If the market price is 5,060 the future is rich by 16.06 points, worth about $803 per ES contract before costs. In practice transaction costs, financing spreads and the cost of trading 500 stocks at once leave a band around fair value; the future can drift inside it.

Roll cost is not lost money. In contango the next contract costs more than the one expiring: with the numbers above, the 3-month versus 6-month spread is about 44 points, or $2,200 per contract per quarter. That is the carry (financing minus dividends) you would also pay if you held the stock instead. It shows up as a drag against spot for a long-only futures investor, but it buys you the financing.

Hedging with futures

  • Beta hedge. To neutralise market exposure of a stock portfolio of value V and beta β using an index future at price F with multiplier m: sell N = βV/(Fm) contracts. A $10M portfolio with beta 1.2 and ES at 5,000 needs 1.2 × 10,000,000/(5,000 × 50) = 48 contracts (480 of the micros).
  • Minimum-variance hedge ratio. If the hedging instrument is imperfectly correlated with what you hold, the variance-minimising ratio is h* = ρσS/σF. Imperfect correlation leaves basis risk.
  • DV01 hedge. For bonds, match risk per basis point. If your book loses $12,000 per 1 bp rise in yields and one Treasury future loses $65 per bp, sell 12,000/65 ≈ 185 contracts.

Rates and bonds

A bond's price is the present value of its cash flows: P = ∑ c/(1 + y)t + 100/(1 + y)N per 100 of face. Price and yield move in opposite directions. The sensitivity is captured by:

  • Macaulay duration: the present-value-weighted average time to the cash flows, in years. A zero-coupon bond's duration equals its maturity. Modified duration = Macaulay/(1 + y/k) is the percentage price change per unit change in yield: ΔP/P ≈ −DmodΔy.
  • DV01 (dollar value of a basis point) = Dmod × P × 0.0001. This is the number desks quote when they say how much risk they carry.
  • Convexity corrects the straight-line approximation: ΔP/P ≈ −DmodΔy + ½C(Δy)2. Convexity is always good for the holder: price rises more when yields fall than it drops when they rise.

Example: a 10-year bond with a 4% annual coupon yielding 4% is priced at 100, has Macaulay duration 8.44 and modified duration 8.11, so its DV01 is 0.0811 per 100 of face, or $811 per $1 million. If yields rise 50 basis points the exact price is 96.04; duration alone says 95.94 and adding convexity gives 96.05.

Bond explorer: price, duration, DV01, convexity

Rates futures. Short-term rate futures are quoted as 100 minus the rate, so a price of 95.50 means 4.50%, and a rise in price means the rate fell. For a 3-month SOFR future, one basis point is worth $25. Treasury futures track a basket of deliverable bonds; the seller chooses the cheapest to deliver, so a future's DV01 is about that bond's DV01 divided by its conversion factor.

Curve trades. A steepener bets that long yields rise relative to short yields: long the 2-year, short the 10-year. To remove the bet on the overall level of rates, make it DV01-neutral. With a semi-annual 4% bond priced at par, the 2-year has a DV01 of about $190 per $1M and the 10-year about $818 per $1M, so you hold 818/190 = 4.29 times as much 2-year as 10-year.

FX essentials

  • Quoting. EURUSD = 1.1000 means 1 euro costs 1.10 dollars: EUR is the base currency, USD the quote currency. A pip is the fourth decimal (0.0001), or the second for yen pairs (0.01).
  • Covered interest parity. The forward rate is set by the interest-rate differential, not by anyone's forecast: F = S(1 + rquoteT)/(1 + rbaseT) (simple interest). The currency with the higher interest rate trades at a forward discount, which exactly cancels the extra interest. With EURUSD at 1.10, USD 5%, EUR 3% and six months, F = 1.1108, so the forward points are +108 pips.
  • Carry trade. Borrow the low-rate currency, lend the high-rate one, and do not hedge. It earns the rate differential as long as spot does not move against you by as much. Historically the high-rate currency has not depreciated as parity would imply on average, which is why the trade exists, and also why it occasionally crashes.
  • Triangular arbitrage. Three exchange rates must be consistent: EURJPY = EURUSD × USDJPY. If the market cross differs from the product, you can cycle through three currencies and end with more than you started.

Risk language

Desks talk about risk in a handful of numbers: delta (dollar exposure to a 1% or 1-unit move), DV01 (rates), vega (volatility), and VaR. Parametric one-day VaR assuming normal returns is VaR = z σdaily V, with z = 1.645 for 95% and 2.326 for 99%. A $10M position with 1% daily volatility has a one-day 99% VaR of $232,600, and the ten-day figure scales by √10 to about $735,500. VaR says nothing about how bad the days beyond the threshold are, and returns have fatter tails than the normal, which is why desks also use stress tests and expected shortfall.

When they ask "how would you hedge this", they want three things in order: what risk you actually have (delta, DV01 or both), what instrument matches it most closely, and what risk you are left with (basis, convexity, correlation).

Solution

Solution

You are long 10 ES contracts at 5,000. How much do you gain or lose for a 1% move in the index, and for a one-tick move?

Solution

Notional = 10 × 5,000 × $50 = $2,500,000, so 1% is $25,000. One tick = 10 × $12.50 = $125. Quoting risk both ways (percent of notional and dollars per tick) is the habit of a desk.

A 3-month SOFR future ($25 per basis point) trades at 95.50. What interest rate does that imply? If you are long and it rises to 95.55, what is your P&L per contract, and what happened to rates?

Solution

Implied rate = 100 − 95.50 = 4.50%. The price rose 0.05, which is 5 basis points, so you earned 5 × $25 = $125, and the implied rate fell to 4.45%. Long a rate future is a bet that rates fall.

A 10-year bond has a 4% annual coupon and yields 4%. Give its price, modified duration and DV01 per $1M of face. Estimate its price if the yield rises 50 basis points, with and without convexity (convexity ≈ 80.8).

Hint

At a yield equal to the coupon, the price is par. Macaulay duration for a par bond with annual coupons is (1 + y)/y × (1 − (1 + y)−N).

Solution

Price 100. Macaulay = 1.04/0.04 × (1 − 1.04−10) = 26 × 0.3244 = 8.435; modified = 8.435/1.04 = 8.11. DV01 = 8.11 × 100 × 0.0001 = 0.0811 per 100, so $811 per $1M. For +50 bp: duration alone gives 100 × (1 − 8.11 × 0.005) = 95.94; with convexity, add ½ × 80.8 × 0.0052 × 100 = 0.10, giving 96.05. The exact price at 4.5% is 96.04. Convexity recovers the missing ten cents.

Your rates book loses $12,000 for each 1 bp rise in yields. A 10-year Treasury future loses about $65 per bp. How many contracts do you sell to be DV01-neutral, and what risks remain?

Solution

12,000/65 ≈ 185 contracts. Remaining: curve risk (your book may be concentrated in other maturities, and the future tracks the 10-year area), convexity (the book and the future may differ in how DV01 changes as yields move), and basis (the cheapest-to-deliver can switch). DV01-neutral is neutral to small parallel moves only.

EURUSD spot is 1.1000. USD rates are 5% and EUR rates 3% (simple interest). Find the six-month forward and the forward points. Which currency is at a forward premium, and why?

Solution

F = 1.1000 × (1 + 0.05 × 0.5)/(1 + 0.03 × 0.5) = 1.1 × 1.025/1.015 = 1.1108, so forward points = +108.4 pips. The euro is at a forward premium because the dollar earns more interest: if the euro did not appreciate in the forward, you could borrow euros, lend dollars, and lock in the difference for free. The shortcut 1.1 × 0.02 × 0.5 = 110 pips is close.

EURUSD = 1.1000 and USDJPY = 150.00, but EURJPY trades at 165.50. Describe the arbitrage and compute the profit per euro, ignoring costs.

Solution

The implied cross is 1.10 × 150 = 165.00, so EURJPY is rich at 165.50. Start with 1 EUR. Sell it for JPY at 165.50 (165.5 yen); sell the yen for dollars at 150 (1.10333 USD); sell the dollars for euros at 1.10 (1.00303 EUR). Profit 0.303% per euro, or €3,030 per €1M, if you could execute all three legs at once. In real markets the profit is smaller than the bid–offer spread on the three legs, which is why these gaps vanish.

A $10M position has daily volatility 1%. What is the one-day 99% parametric VaR, and the ten-day figure? What does it not tell you?

Solution

1-day: 2.326 × 1% × $10M = $232,600. Ten-day, assuming independent days: ×√10 = $735,500. It says nothing about how large losses are when the threshold is breached (a 1% tail event could be twice VaR or ten times), assumes normal returns when tails are fat, and assumes volatility is constant when it clusters. Hence stress tests and expected shortfall.

You want a steepener: long the 2-year, short the 10-year, DV01-neutral. Using DV01s of $190 per $1M (2-year) and $818 per $1M (10-year), how much 2-year do you hold per $1M of 10-year? If the curve steepens by 10 bp (the 10-year yield rises 5 bp, the 2-year falls 5 bp), what do you make? What if both yields rise 20 bp?

Solution

Hold 818/190 = 4.29 times the notional: $4.29M of 2-year per $1M of 10-year, so both legs have DV01 about $818. The short 10-year gains $818 × 5 = $4,090 and the long 2-year gains $818 × 5 = $4,090: total about $8,180 (that is, DV01 × the 10 bp change in the spread). For a 20 bp parallel rise the legs offset: the short 10-year gains $818 × 20 and the long 2-year loses the same: about zero, ignoring carry and convexity.

How a trader interview actually goes

A typical first-round trading interview lasts thirty to forty-five minutes and has a recognisable shape: a few minutes of mental arithmetic as a warm-up, a probability or expected-value puzzle, a market-making or betting game, sometimes a brainteaser, and a short conversation about why you want to trade. The puzzle is rarely a single question. It escalates: the interviewer changes a number, adds a cost, asks for a simulation, or asks why. The first question tells them whether you know the technique. The follow-ups tell them whether you understand it.

What they are scoring, in roughly this order:

  1. Did you set the problem up correctly? Stated assumptions, the right model, the right quantity.
  2. Did you reach a right answer at a sensible speed?
  3. Did you notice your own errors? Catching and fixing a slip is a positive signal, not a negative one.
  4. How did you handle new information? The escalation is a test of adaptability.
  5. Could they trade next to you? Clear, calm, honest about uncertainty.

The four habits

Before you compute

State your assumptions. "I'll treat the die as fair and the rolls as independent." Five seconds that prevent a minute of confusion.

Estimate first. "I'd guess around 4, so let me check." The estimate is a safety net for the exact calculation.

While you compute

Keep talking. Say what you are doing even when it is mechanical. Silence reads as stuck.

Check the size. A probability above 1, a negative variance and an expected value outside the possible range are all red flags you should catch before they do.

A random interview

Three chains chosen at random, graded by you. Speak every answer aloud (record yourself once, and listen for the silences and the filler).

The test-day playbook

Two passes

On the first pass, answer everything you can finish in under about 1.5 times the average time per question, and skip the rest. On the second, spend the remaining time on the skipped questions in order of how close you were. The daily 90 minutes

Fifteen minutes of gym; thirty minutes of new material with pencil in hand; thirty minutes of problems against the clock; ten minutes of one interview chain out loud; five minutes writing the error log. Never skip the log: the act of writing the rule you violated is what makes it stick.

When the test turns into an interview

  • Think out loud. The interviewer is grading your process. Silence looks like being stuck even when you are not.
  • State the model. "I'll treat the dice as independent and fair, so the expectation is..." costs five seconds and prevents a misunderstanding.
  • Estimate first, then refine. Offering "somewhere around 30%" before the exact calculation shows judgment and gives you a check on the final answer.
  • Ask a clarifying question when something is ambiguous (is the die fair; is the coin independent; with or without replacement). Ambiguity resolved well is a strength.
  • Admit and fix errors quickly. "That's wrong, because the events are not independent; let me redo it" is the single most trusted sentence in the building.
  • In market-making games always quote. Update on every trade out loud: "you bought at 15, so I think the value is higher than I thought; my new market is 15.5 at 17."

  • Zetamac, an online arithmetic game: the standard for mental-math speed.

  • Project Euler: mathematical programming problems.

  • LeetCode, HackerRank, Codeforces: coding practice by pattern.
  • Past candidate reports (forums and interview-prep sites) for your target firm, read for format and not as an answer key, since formats change.
  • A study partner: play the market-making game and the dice and card games with a friend. Nothing trains out-loud reasoning like an opponent.
